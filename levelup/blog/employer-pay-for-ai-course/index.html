How to get your employer to pay for an AI course (with an email template)

Most people who take a paid course at work never ask their employer to cover it. That is often a mistake. A lot of companies set aside money for training, and an AI course that ends with something working inside your job is one of the easier requests to approve, if you frame it the right way.

Why this request is easier than it looks

Your manager is not really being asked to "pay for a course." They are being asked to spend a known amount of money on a specific result for the team. That is a decision they make all the time, about software, contractors and conferences.

The requests that get turned down tend to share a pattern. They are vague ("I want to learn AI"), they arrive at the wrong time (after the budget is spent), or they go to the wrong person (someone who cares about headcount, not training). Fix those three things and your odds go up a lot.

This guide walks through the full process: building the case, picking who to ask, choosing the timing, sending the email, handling a "no," and dealing with invoices once it is approved.

Step 1: Pick one problem the course will solve

Before you write anything, decide what you will build. Not "get better at AI." One specific piece of work in your job that is slow, repetitive or error-prone.

Good examples are concrete and visible to your manager:

  • Sorting and replying to routine customer or supplier emails
  • Pulling numbers from invoices or receipts into a spreadsheet
  • Writing the same weekly status report from four different tools
  • Turning meeting notes into actions, owners and follow-up emails
  • Researching prospects before sales calls

If you are not sure which one to pick, list everything you did last week that you would happily never do again. Circle the one that took the most hours and followed the most predictable steps. That is your candidate.

Step 2: Do the hours math (honestly)

Managers respond to time and money. You do not need a spreadsheet model, but you do need a rough, defensible estimate. Here is a worked example with hypothetical numbers. Replace every figure with your own.

ItemHypothetical exampleHow to get your real number
Hours per week on the task4 hoursTrack it for one week, honestly
People doing similar work3 peopleAsk teammates, or check your team's process docs
Share of the task you expect to automateHalfBe conservative; humans still check the output
Hours freed per week4 × 3 × 0.5 = 6 hoursMultiply the three lines above
Hours freed over a year (about 46 working weeks)About 276 hoursUse your company's working weeks

Then compare that to the cost. An Level Up flagship track is US$897 at the early-bird price (about HK$6,999) or US$1,200 standard. In the example above, the course only needs to free a small fraction of those hours for the math to work.

Two rules keep this credible:

  1. Underestimate. If you think you will automate 70%, write 50%. A manager who catches one inflated number stops trusting the rest.
  2. Say what stays human. "The workflow drafts, I review and send" is more believable than "AI handles it." It also answers the risk question before it is asked.

You are not promising savings. You are showing that the request is in proportion to the size of the problem.

Step 3: Find the right person to ask

Your direct manager is usually the first stop, but they are often not the person who holds the money. Knowing who cares about what helps you shape the message.

WhoWhat they usually care aboutWhat to give them
Your direct managerTeam output, deadlines, whether you will be distractedThe specific task, the time commitment, the plan to share what you build
Department headBudget, priorities, how this compares to other requestsThe hours math and the total cost in one line
HR or L&D (learning and development)Training policy, eligibility, paperwork, fair accessCourse details, dates, the provider's invoice and refund terms
FinanceCorrect invoice, cost center, payment timingAn invoice addressed to the company, currency, due date
Founder or owner (small company)Return on spend, speed, whether it helps the whole businessWhat you will build and how others on the team can reuse it

In a small business, one conversation with the owner often settles it. In a larger company, your manager approves the idea and HR or L&D handles the money. Ask your manager early: "If you're supportive, who else needs to sign off?"

Step 4: Time the ask

When you ask matters almost as much as what you ask. A few patterns are common across companies:

  • Before the budget is set. Many companies plan next year's training spend in the last quarter. If your financial year starts in January, November and December are good months to get on the list.
  • Right after the budget is released. Early in a budget period, training money is often unspent and managers have more room to say yes.
  • Near the end of a budget period. Some teams have leftover training budget that they lose if they do not use it. Your manager may welcome a well-formed request.
  • During performance reviews. If your review includes development goals, put the course in as one of them. It turns the request into something already agreed.

Avoid the week of a major deadline, a reorganization or a hiring freeze announcement. Not because the answer will always be no, but because nobody will read your email carefully.

Step 5: Send the email

This is the template from our landing page, lightly expanded. Fill in the brackets and cut anything that does not fit your situation. Keep it short. One screen is enough.

Subject: Request to attend Level Up's AI training (January 2027)

Hi [Manager's name],

I'd like to join Level Up's live AI cohort, [AI for Automation / AI for Entrepreneurs], starting [start date]. It runs for four weeks with eight live sessions, about four to five hours a week, mostly outside core hours.

Why it matters for our team:
- I'll build and ship one real AI workflow for [specific task or process] by the final session
- I expect it to save around [X] hours a week across [team / process]
- I'll share what I build and a short write-up with the team afterwards

Cost: [amount and currency] per person, including replays and a certificate. There is a full refund up to the halfway point.

Could this come from our training or professional development budget? I can send an invoice addressed to the company.

Thanks,
[Your name]

A few notes on why it is written this way:

  • The first line says what you want. No warm-up paragraph.
  • The benefit is about the team, not your personal growth. Your growth matters, but it is not what the budget is for.
  • The time cost is stated up front. Most managers' first worry is whether your work will slip.
  • The risk is addressed. A refund up to the halfway point (the end of session 4 on a flagship track) means the company is not locked in if it turns out to be a poor fit.
  • It ends with a yes or no question that is easy to answer.

Step 6: If your manager says no

A "no" is often a "not like this" or "not right now." Ask which one it is. Then try the follow-up below, a week or so later, once you have done some of the work on your own.

Subject: Re: AI training request, a smaller version

Hi [Manager's name],

Thanks for considering the course. I understand [the budget is tight / the timing is hard].

I've started mapping [task] on my own. Last week it took me about [X] hours, and I think a good part of that could be drafted automatically, with me checking every output before it goes anywhere.

Two options that might work better:
1. The company covers part of the cost, and I cover the rest.
2. I take the course on my own time and money, and we revisit reimbursement once I show you a working version.

Either way, I'd like to try this on [task] and report back by [date].

Thanks,
[Your name]

This works for three reasons. It shows you are serious enough to start without permission. It gives your manager a smaller decision to make. And it sets up a later conversation where you can show something real instead of describing it.

If the answer is still no, decide whether to pay yourself. Some people do. Some companies reimburse after completion even when they will not pay in advance. Check your employee handbook or ask HR whether that option exists.

Step 7: Once it is approved, sort out the paperwork

Finance teams like clean paperwork. Make it easy for them:

  1. Request a company invoice. Level Up can issue an invoice addressed to your company. Ask for it from the employer section of the landing page, or message the team on WhatsApp.
  2. Confirm the currency. You can pay in USD, HKD, SGD, AED, GBP, CAD or PHP. Pick the one your finance team prefers so nobody has to deal with conversion questions.
  3. Check timing against the early-bird price. If approval takes weeks, tell your manager the early-bird price is US$897 and the standard price is US$1,200. That difference is sometimes enough to speed things up.
  4. Write down the refund terms. Full refund up to the halfway point, then the outcome guarantee: do the work and do not ship your track's deliverable by session 8, and you get your money back. Finance will want to know this exists.
  5. Agree how you will report back. A short write-up and a demo to the team after session 8 is usually enough.

Step 8: Deliver on what you promised

The best way to make the next request easy, for you or a colleague, is to come back with something that works.

  • Pick the task before session 1, so week one is spent on your real work and not on choosing an idea.
  • Keep a simple log of hours spent on the task before and after. Real numbers beat any estimate in the original email.
  • Show your manager the workflow running, including where a human still checks it.
  • Share the write-up with the team so others can reuse it.

If your company has several people interested, ask about running a private cohort for your team. That is often simpler for HR than approving individual requests one at a time.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Asking for "AI training" in general. Too vague to approve.
  • Leading with the certificate. A certificate is nice to have, but the result for the team is what gets funded.
  • Inflating the savings. One bad number undermines the whole request.
  • Ignoring data rules. If your task involves customer data, say you will check what tools are allowed before building anything. That one sentence removes a common objection.
  • Asking at the last minute. Approvals and invoices take time. Ask at least three to four weeks before the start date.
